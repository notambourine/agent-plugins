# Surgical edit

Change phrasing only. Keep every fact, number, name, citation, and claim, and keep the
structure, the register, and any choice the author would defend. When you are unsure
whether something is a tell or the author's voice, leave it. Flattening a good sentence
does more harm than leaving one tell in.

## Steps

1. Read the whole piece and note its voice.
2. Collect candidates without editing.
3. Check each one: would cutting it lose meaning, and is it a formula or a choice?
4. Apply the edits that remain, each as the smallest change in phrasing.
5. Reread the edited passages and fix any that now read choppy or flat.
6. Report what changed and the category of each tell.

When drafting, write first and run these steps on the draft afterward.

## Tells

| Tell | Fix |
| --- | --- |
| Announcing the point before making it ("It is worth noting that") | Delete the frame and state the point. |
| A vague "This" or "That" verdict after a sentence | Cut it, or fold the idea into the sentence. |
| Intensifiers and puffery ("truly", "fundamentally") | Delete. |
| Telling the reader a point matters | Show the consequence. |
| A metaphor written to sound clever | State it plainly. |
| A grand prediction | Cut it, or scope it to a claim you can defend. |
| A list padded to three items | Keep the items that are true. |
| "Not X, but Y" repeated through the piece | Keep the one that carries the argument and rewrite the rest. |
| "Not only... but also" | Split or simplify. |
| Hedging that commits to nothing | Take the position or cut the sentence. |
| A closing sentence that restates the paragraph | Cut it. |
| Overused AI vocabulary | Use the plain word or cut. |

Keep a short fragment that earns its place after a long sentence, deliberate parallelism,
a strong closing line, and first-person conviction the author owns. Stop when the
remaining candidates are voice. Never replace one formula with another. For a contested
edit, read [references/tells.md](references/tells.md).
